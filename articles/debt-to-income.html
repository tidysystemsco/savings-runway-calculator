The Debt-to-Income Ratio That Actually Matters

Two people can earn the exact same salary and be in completely different financial danger zones. The number that actually explains the difference is debt-to-income, not income itself.

DTI = total monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income. Under 36% is generally considered safe. 36-43% is a caution zone. 43%+ is a red flag to most lenders — even when income looks strong on paper.

What counts as "debt payments" in this ratio

This includes recurring debt obligations: mortgage or rent, car payments, minimum credit card payments, student loans, and any other loan payments. It does not include your other living expenses like groceries or utilities — this ratio is specifically about debt obligations against gross (pre-tax) income.

Why lenders lean on this number so heavily

DTI is a standardized way to estimate how much financial cushion someone has before debt payments consume their income, regardless of what that income actually is. A high earner with a high DTI can represent more real risk to a lender than a modest earner with a low one, which is why this ratio often matters more than the salary figure alone during loan approval.

Why it's worth knowing even if you're not applying for anything

Your DTI is a useful personal early-warning number independent of any lender. Watching it trend up over time — even before it crosses into caution territory — is a signal worth acting on before it becomes a real constraint on what you can borrow, or a sign that new debt is quietly displacing your own financial flexibility.
